# Key ideas from April Dunford

April Dunford is a positioning consultant who worked with dozens of B2B startups before writing it up as *Obviously Awesome* (2019) and *Sales Pitch* (2023). Her core claim: positioning isn't branding or messaging — it's the foundation those sit on, and most companies get it wrong without realizing it's even a distinct problem to solve.

## Positioning is the foundation, not decoration

Dunford treats positioning as context-setting: it decides how a product is understood before a single word of marketing copy gets written. Get it wrong, and brand, messaging, and sales collateral built on top are all compromised, no matter how well-executed they are.

## The five components of positioning

Her framework (from *Obviously Awesome*) runs in a fixed order, because each step depends on the one before it:

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would the customer do if your product didn't exist? This is usually broader than "direct competitors" — it often includes a spreadsheet, a manual process, a junior hire, or simply doing nothing. Founders frequently forget "do nothing," even though it's often the most common alternative.
2. **Unique attributes** — what capabilities do you have that those alternatives don't?
3. **Value themes** — group those attributes into two to four themes describing the value they create for the customer. A long feature list is not positioning.
4. **Best-fit customers** — for whom is that value most acute? This defines your ideal customer profile.
5. **Market category** — given the alternatives and the best-fit buyer, what existing category frames the product most favorably?

Her rule of thumb: you only get one positioning. Trying to be "all things to all buyers" is the same as having no positioning at all.

## Stop comparing to competitors — compare to alternatives

A recurring Dunford point: companies obsess over direct competitors and miss that the real competition is often inertia — a customer doing nothing, or solving the problem with a spreadsheet. Positioning that only addresses other vendors misses the biggest category of lost deals.

## Marketing pitch vs. sales pitch

In *Sales Pitch*, she draws a sharp line: the marketing pitch earns the meeting, the sales pitch wins the deal, and they're built differently. A good sales pitch opens with a non-obvious insight about the buyer's world — not a product feature — then walks through the alternatives and their honest tradeoffs, paints a "perfect world" picture, and only then introduces the differentiated value, backed by proof, with a real reason to act now rather than later.

## Category creation is a trap for most companies

Dunford is skeptical of "create a new category" advice, which she considers roughly ten times harder than entering an existing category and re-segmenting it in your favor. Most category-creation success stories are survivorship bias; the default move should be to find an existing category the buyer already understands and win a sub-segment of it.

## Repositioning is a project, not a tagline

Signs that a company needs to reposition, per Dunford: sales cycles quietly lengthening, losing deals to weaker competitors, prospects asking "so what do you actually do?" after a demo, or sales reps describing the product differently from one another. Her point is that these are symptoms of a positioning problem, and the fix is a structured project — reworking the five components — not a copywriting pass.

## Why this matters

Dunford's framework gives founders and product/marketing teams a repeatable diagnostic for "why aren't deals closing" that goes upstream of messaging — often the real problem is that the company hasn't decided what it's being compared to, or for whom it's obviously the right choice.
