# Key ideas from David Ogilvy

David Ogilvy (1911–1999) founded Ogilvy & Mather and is widely credited as a father of modern advertising, known for campaigns like Rolls-Royce, Hathaway shirts, Schweppes, and Dove. His two books, *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963) and *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983), codified a research-first, specifics-over-cleverness approach that's still taught in marketing programs today.

## "If it doesn't sell, it isn't creative"

Ogilvy's most repeated principle: advertising isn't art, and the only honest judge of an ad is whether it moves the cash register. He was openly hostile to industry awards for "creativity" that didn't correlate with sales, and treated direct-response advertising — where results are measurable — as the discipline's truth-test. His view was that every general advertiser should spend time in direct response, because it tells you what the consumer actually does, not what she says she'd do in a focus group.

## Research before copy

Ogilvy's rule: never write a word of copy before studying the product, the customer, and the competition. His training process (the "Magic Lantern" sequence) started with research, then identified the product's actual mechanism — what makes the promise true — before drafting a headline. He treated skipping research as close to a fireable offense in his agency.

## Respect the reader

"The consumer isn't a moron, she's your wife" was his way of insisting copywriters talk to readers as intelligent equals rather than talking down to them.

## The Big Idea

Ogilvy's test for whether an ad actually works: read it once as a stranger would. Does it (1) stop you, (2) communicate the proposition clearly, and (3) persuade you to act or believe something? If any of the three fails, you don't have a Big Idea — you have a paragraph. He argued most advertising fails this test, however polished it looks.

## The headline does most of the work

Ogilvy's estimate was that roughly 80% of readers never get past the headline — so the headline effectively is the ad. His own famous example is the 1958 Rolls-Royce ad whose headline said that at 60 miles an hour, the loudest sound in the car was the electric clock. It was a headline built entirely from a specific, verifiable detail rather than a vague superlative.

## Specifics beat generalities

A recurring Ogilvy instruction: count the specifics in a piece of copy — named numbers, demonstrations, concrete details. Vague claims ("best," "world's finest") persuade no one; he treated superlatives and generic claims as evidence the writer hadn't done the research or found the real selling point.

## What he told writers to avoid

Jingles (they sing past the actual proposition), irrelevant celebrity endorsements ("borrowed interest" — people remember the celebrity, not the product), and cleverness for its own sake, which he considered a kind of vandalism: the reader admires the wit and forgets what was being sold.

## Brand image is built over decades, one ad at a time

Ogilvy treated every single ad as either a deposit into, or a withdrawal from, a brand's long-term image — and argued the campaigns that worked (Schweppes' "Commander Whitehead," the Hathaway shirt's eyepatch, Dove's "one-quarter cleansing cream") held the same image consistently for years rather than reinventing themselves campaign to campaign.

## The taste floor

"Never write an ad you wouldn't want your family to read" was his baseline standard — a floor below which no amount of effectiveness justified running the work.

## Why this matters

Ogilvy's frame is still a useful checklist for anyone writing persuasive copy of any kind: has the research actually been done, is there one real idea (not just polish), does the headline carry a specific and true claim, and does the work build toward a consistent image over time rather than chasing a clever one-off.
