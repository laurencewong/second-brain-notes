# Key ideas from Naval Ravikant

Naval Ravikant is an entrepreneur and investor — co-founder of AngelList, and an early investor in companies like Twitter and Uber — whose ideas on wealth, leverage, and decision-making spread largely through a viral 2018 Twitter thread, "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)," and were later compiled by Eric Jorgenson into *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* (2020).

## Wealth, not money or status

Naval's starting distinction is between wealth (assets that earn for you even while you're not working), money (a transfer mechanism for wealth), and status (a zero-sum social ranking). His argument is that most people chase money or status, when the thing actually worth optimizing for is wealth — in his definition, because wealth compounds and status, by its zero-sum nature, can't.

## Specific knowledge

Naval describes "specific knowledge" as the kind of expertise that can't be taught through formal training because it's built from genuine curiosity and ability rather than credentialing. He locates it at the overlap of three things: what you're genuinely curious about (not performatively), what comes more naturally to you than to most people, and what society doesn't have an established pipeline for training someone else to do. His claim is that stacking two or three such areas together makes you difficult to replace — and that this intersection, not a job title, is the real asset to build.

## The four kinds of leverage

A central framework from the 2018 thread: there are four forms of leverage — labor, capital, code, and media. Labor and capital require permission (a boss, an investor) and are capped by hours or access to money. Code and media, Naval argues, are "permissionless" — you can produce them without anyone's approval, they have near-zero marginal cost to reproduce, and they can keep working or compounding attention while you sleep. His recurring advice: look for ways to shift your work toward the leverage types that don't require your continued hourly presence.

## Productize yourself

Naval's test for whether you've actually built an asset rather than just labor: "if you stopped showing up tomorrow, would it still produce?" If yes, you've productized your knowledge into something durable (a book, code, a brand, a product). If no, you're renting out your hours, even if you're nominally self-employed. He frames this as the real goal of turning specific knowledge into leverage.

## Play long-term games with long-term people

Naval frequently returns to the idea that most of the returns in life — in capital, relationships, and reputation — come from compound interest, which requires playing "iterated games": situations where you'll encounter the same people again, so trust and reputation can accumulate. His practical test before entering any deal, relationship, or project: will I see these people again, and does winning this round set up the next one, or does the scoreboard reset? If the scoreboard resets (a one-shot interaction with a stranger), he argues the return is inherently bounded and reputation shouldn't be spent cheaply there.

## Decision speed depends on reversibility

Borrowing and adapting a concept associated with Amazon's "two-way door" decisions, Naval distinguishes reversible decisions (where the cost of being wrong is just the cost of undoing it, so you should decide quickly) from irreversible ones (marriage, having kids, large public commitments — where slow deliberation is justified). His observation is that people often misclassify reversible decisions as irreversible and over-deliberate them, wasting time that should go to moving fast.

## Read what you love until you love to read

Naval treats reading broadly as the highest-leverage compounding habit available, but cautions against forcing yourself through books you feel you "should" read. His suggestion is to follow genuine interest — reading whatever holds your attention — until reading itself becomes a sustained habit, and only then "upgrade" to denser material.

## What money can't buy

Naval often closes his framework by noting that the things people actually want — "a calm mind, a fit body, a house full of love" — aren't for sale, and that free time (not having to think about money) is the highest-status asset precisely because so few people who are wealthy by his definition still have much of it.

**Sources:** "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" Twitter thread (2018); *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* (Eric Jorgenson, 2020); various podcast appearances including The Joe Rogan Experience and The Tim Ferriss Show.
