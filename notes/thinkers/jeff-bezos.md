# Key ideas from Jeff Bezos

Jeff Bezos founded Amazon in 1994 and ran it as CEO until 2021, writing an annual shareholder letter nearly every year of that run. Those letters — later anthologized along with other writing and speeches in *Invent and Wander* (2021) — are the primary public record of a specific, fairly consistent philosophy about decision-making, organizational discipline, and long-term thinking.

## "It's always Day 1"

The line that opens Bezos's first (1997) shareholder letter and that he returned to for the rest of his tenure. His argument: "Day 2" is stasis, followed eventually by irrelevance and decline, and the entire discipline of running a growing company is actively resisting the pull toward Day 2 complacency. In a later letter (2016), he laid out four things he associated with staying in Day 1: genuine customer obsession rather than competitor obsession, skepticism toward process as a substitute for actual outcomes, eagerly adopting large external trends rather than fighting them, and high-velocity decision-making.

## Customer obsession over competitor obsession

Bezos repeatedly argued that competitors don't pay you — customers do — and that an organization fixated on what rivals are doing tends to drift from what actually matters. He described customers as being usefully, "divinely" dissatisfied even when they say they're happy, which he treated as a permanent engine for improvement rather than something to be fixed.

## Two-way doors and one-way doors

A distinction from his 2015 letter: most business decisions are "two-way doors" — reversible. If you're wrong, you can just walk back through. Those decisions should be made quickly, by individuals or small groups, without waiting for more information. A smaller set of decisions are "one-way doors" — effectively irreversible — and those deserve slow, careful, consultative deliberation. Bezos argued the most common organizational mistake is treating reversible decisions as if they were irreversible, which creates needless organizational slowness as decision-making processes designed for genuinely high-stakes calls get applied to routine ones.

## Deciding with 70% of the information

Related to the door distinction: Bezos argued that most decisions should be made with something like 70% of the information you'd ideally want, because waiting for 90% costs more in lost time than the additional 20% of information is typically worth — especially for reversible decisions.

## Disagree and commit

When a senior person disagrees with a decision that's already been made, Bezos's model is for them to state that disagreement clearly and on the record, and then commit fully to executing the decision as though they'd agreed. The point, in his framing, is to preserve both decision speed and genuine organizational alignment — the failure mode it's meant to prevent is quietly agreeing in the room and then sabotaging or dragging on execution afterward.

## Working backward from the press release

A product-development practice attributed to internal Amazon process: before building anything, write the press release announcing the finished product, plus an internal FAQ that's honest about what's hard. Iterate on both documents until the press release is genuinely exciting and the FAQ doesn't dodge the hard questions. Only then build. Bezos has described this less as a planning document and more as a forcing function — many projects, in his account, die appropriately at this stage, because trying to write an exciting press release reveals that the underlying idea isn't actually compelling.

## Six-page memos instead of slide decks

Starting around 2017–2018, Bezos moved Amazon's internal decision meetings away from PowerPoint decks toward six-page narrative memos written in full sentences, with the first twenty minutes of any meeting spent reading the memo silently in the room. His reasoning: full sentences force the author to actually know what they think, where bullet points can hide incomplete thinking; and silent reading puts everyone in the room at the same level of context before discussion starts.

## Bets should scale with the company

Bezos argued that if a company's mistakes stay small as the company grows, that's itself a sign the company is playing too cautiously — the size of the bets, and therefore the size of the occasional misses, needs to scale up along with the business for genuine invention to keep happening.

## Build for what won't change

Bezos has said that asking "what's not going to change in the next ten years" is often more useful than asking what will change. For Amazon, he identified durable customer preferences — lower prices, more selection, faster delivery — as the stable foundation worth building heavy infrastructure around, reserving fast, cheap experimentation for the parts of the business that were genuinely likely to shift.

**Sources:** Amazon shareholder letters, 1997–2020, written by Jeff Bezos; *Invent and Wander: The Collected Writings of Jeff Bezos* (2021).
