# Key ideas from Richard Feynman

Richard Feynman (1918–1988) was a theoretical physicist and Nobel laureate (1965, for work on quantum electrodynamics) who worked on the Manhattan Project, taught at Caltech for decades, and served on the Rogers Commission investigating the 1986 *Challenger* disaster. Beyond his physics, Feynman is widely read for his writing and talks on how to actually understand something, and on the discipline of not fooling yourself.

## The first principle is not to fool yourself

Feynman's most quoted line, from his 1974 Caltech commencement address "Cargo Cult Science," is that you must not fool yourself, "and you are the easiest person to fool." His argument is that intellectual honesty with yourself is harder than honesty with others, because self-deception doesn't feel like deception from the inside — which is why it needs active, structured defenses rather than good intentions.

## Cargo cult science

In that same address, Feynman coined "cargo cult science" to describe work that goes through all the external motions of rigor — citations, jargon, conferences, the "form" of science — without the underlying integrity check that the work could actually be wrong. He offered a short set of questions for testing whether work is rigorous or merely performing rigor:

- Did you report *all* the data, or only the data supporting your conclusion?
- Have you named what observation would prove you wrong?
- Are you doing the substance, or just the form (the citations and jargon)?
- Would you publish a result that disconfirmed your own hypothesis?

## Understanding vs. recognizing

Feynman's test for real understanding, often summarized from a line on his blackboard at the time of his death ("What I cannot create, I do not understand"), is whether you can build or derive something from scratch — not just recognize or recite it. He treated the gap between "I can repeat the explanation" and "I could construct this myself" as the place most false confidence hides.

## The Feynman Technique (teach-back)

A widely popularized method attributed to Feynman's teaching style works like this:
1. Pick the concept you think you understand.
2. Explain it out loud, in plain language, as if to someone with no background — no jargon allowed.
3. Notice where you hand-wave, say "basically" or "obviously," or get stuck — those are the actual gaps.
4. Go back to primary material to fill exactly those gaps.
5. Simplify and repeat until the explanation is clean.

The underlying test isn't "does this sound right" but "could someone without the background accurately repeat it back."

## Reduce it to a picture

Feynman repeatedly described his own method for working through physics as reducing an abstraction to a concrete picture — actual objects, actual relationships, actual sequences of cause and effect — and then rebuilding the conclusion from that picture without leaning on the abstract vocabulary. If the conclusion falls apart once you remove the jargon, he treated that as evidence the jargon had been standing in for understanding rather than describing it.

## Skepticism of authority

In a 1966 address, "What is Science?," Feynman described science as "the belief in the ignorance of experts" — meaning that credentials and authority aren't themselves evidence, and that a claim should be evaluated on the reasoning and evidence behind it rather than on who's saying it. He paired this with comfort about not knowing things: in a 1981 BBC interview he said he didn't feel frightened by not knowing — treating comfort with uncertainty as a precondition for actually finding things out, rather than a weakness to paper over with false confidence.

## Reality over public relations

As part of the Rogers Commission investigating the *Challenger* space shuttle disaster, Feynman famously demonstrated with a glass of ice water how the shuttle's O-ring material lost resilience at low temperatures — a simple, physical demonstration that cut through the more abstract institutional debate. His appendix to the commission's report closed with a now-famous line arguing that, for a technology to succeed, reality has to come before public relations, because "nature cannot be fooled." It's become a shorthand for his broader argument that organizational incentives to look good can quietly override the discipline of checking what's actually true.

## Ordinary fools vs. pompous fools

From his memoir *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985), Feynman drew a distinction between people who are simply wrong and admit it, and people who hide uncertainty behind credentials, jargon, or status. He treated the second group as the more dangerous one — not because they know less, but because their presentation discourages anyone (including themselves) from checking.
