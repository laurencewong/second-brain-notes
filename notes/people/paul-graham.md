# Paul Graham

Programmer, essayist, and investor. Co-founded Viaweb, one of the first web-based e-commerce platforms, which was acquired by Yahoo in 1998 and became Yahoo Store. Co-founded Y Combinator with Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris, and Trevor Blackwell in 2005, and ran it as partner and later president through roughly 2013–2014. Writes one of the most widely cited essay collections on startups and technology (published at paulgraham.com) and is a vocal advocate for the Lisp programming language. In 2024, he wrote the essay "Founder Mode," which popularized Brian Chesky's framing of the same name.

## Why he matters

Graham is widely credited with shaping the modern startup-accelerator model through Y Combinator, and his essays — written in plain, aphoristic prose — are frequently cited as some of the most influential writing in startup culture, covering everything from how to generate startup ideas to how founders should manage their time.

## Key essays and ideas

**"How to Get Startup Ideas" (2012).** Argues the best startup ideas come from founders noticing a problem they personally have, not from deliberately brainstorming "startup-shaped" ideas. Warns against ideas that merely look like startups (a marketplace for X, a SaaS for Y) without a real founder-problem connection, and against "tar pits" — ideas that look appealing on the surface but don't exist because the underlying problem is harder than it looks.

**"Do Things That Don't Scale" (2013).** Argues the earliest stage of a company isn't a scaled-down version of a mature company — it's structurally different work. Founders should hand-recruit their first users, deliver service manually, and get "insultingly" close to early customers, rather than trying to build scalable systems before they have anything worth scaling.

**"Default Alive or Default Dead?" (2015).** A simple test for startup survival: at current burn rate and growth, will the company reach profitability before it runs out of money? If yes, it's "default alive"; if no, "default dead" — regardless of how things feel day to day. Graham argues founders are systematically overoptimistic about which one they are.

**"Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" (2009).** Observes that people who make things (writers, programmers) need long uninterrupted blocks of time, while managers naturally work in short meeting-sized slots — and that a single 30-minute meeting dropped into a maker's morning can destroy the whole block.

**"The Schlep Blueprint" (2012).** Argues founders systematically avoid "schleps" — tedious, unglamorous, bureaucratic work — which creates opportunity for those willing to do it. Cites Stripe (payments processing, a regulatory- and integration-heavy schlep most founders avoided) as the canonical example.

**"Founder Mode" (2024).** Named and popularized the operating philosophy Brian Chesky had already articulated in a Y Combinator talk — that founder-led companies often do better under direct, skip-level founder involvement than under conventional "hire great people and delegate" management orthodoxy.

## Y Combinator's model

Under Graham, YC ran deliberately small batches (early batches had around eight startups), held weekly "office hours" built around the question "what's the most important problem you're facing this week," and used a fixed Demo Day as a forcing function for startups to have something to show. Graham has argued YC's most durable asset wasn't capital or selection skill but its alumni community, and summarized the whole philosophy in a single phrase: "Make something people want."

## Career timeline

| Period | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1995–1998 | Built and sold Viaweb (became Yahoo Store) |
| 1999–2004 | Essayist; wrote foundational pieces including "Hackers and Painters" |
| 2005–2013 | Y Combinator partner, then president; wrote most of his best-known operational essays |
| 2014–2024 | Post-YC essayist and occasional investor; wrote "Default Alive or Default Dead?" |
| 2024– | Wrote "Founder Mode," re-engaging with startup-operating commentary |
