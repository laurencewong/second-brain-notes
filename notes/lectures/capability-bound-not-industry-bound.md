# Capability-bound, not industry-bound

**Source:** [All-In Podcast — Charles & Chase Koch interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EIo3AuyvV84)

**The idea.** In a 2026 interview, Charles Koch described the principle that let his family turn a refinery business into a $150B private company: define the company by what it's *capable of*, not by what industry it happens to sit in. Most companies do the opposite — "we're an oil company," "we're a fintech" — and that label quietly becomes a cage.

**Why it works.** Koch Industries treated itself as an integrated bundle of capabilities — operations, logistics, trading, a particular management philosophy — and treated the industry as just wherever that bundle currently got deployed. Underneath is an old idea: comparative advantage. Find the slice of any value chain where your bundle of capabilities creates more value than anyone else's, operate there, and don't feel obligated to stay inside the category you started in.

**How it played out.** Koch's actual trajectory tracks the capability, not the category: crude oil gathering, then natural gas, then chemicals, then fertilizers, then consumer products (the 2005 Georgia-Pacific acquisition). Each move redeployed the same underlying engine into a new industry where it had an edge. Tellingly, the exit rule was symmetric to the entry rule: leave a business not when results dip, but when you conclude you no longer have the capability to create value a customer will actually pay a premium for. That's a structural test, not an emotional one.

**Why this is rare.** Public markets mostly forbid this way of thinking. Analysts and indices are built to price *industries* — there's no clean box for "an integrated set of capabilities that moves between industries." A public company that tried this would likely be pressured to break itself into legible, single-industry pieces. That's part of why capability-bound thinking shows up most in private companies, family-controlled businesses, and individual operators — anyone who doesn't have to be legible to outside analysts.

**The catch.** Capability-bound doesn't mean capability-*restless*. Koch stayed in oil and gas for years before its first adjacent move. Capabilities deepen the longer you sit with the context that built them, and jumping to a new "wrapper" too early throws away exactly the contextual knowledge that made the capability sharp in the first place. The frame is for recognizing what you actually own — not an excuse to keep hopping.

**A quick test.** Try describing your own work or organization purely in terms of capabilities rather than industry labels. If the next move becomes obvious once you do that, the frame is doing real work. If nothing becomes clearer, you probably haven't found the actual engine yet — you're still describing the chassis it happens to be riding in.
